Editor's note

This month's papers circle one theme: how the US dollar, US Treasuries and US policy set the tone for everyone else. Two papers examine the dollar's safe-haven status, one through the April 2025 tariff episode and one through a model in which US tightening raises the global price of risk. A further study shows US bond supply spilling over into foreign yields and output. A second group asks how uncertainty is priced, covering monetary-policy risk, euro area inflation risks and multiple equilibria in sovereign debt, while the European sovereign-bank evidence shows that fiscal stress still reaches balance sheets. Finally, stablecoin flows suggest that dollarisation in emerging markets may be taking a new form.

DM  G10 central bank  ·  Deutsche Bundesbank  ·  30 Oct 2026

Tariff-era dollar weakness reflected several forces, not a single tariff shock

What volatility reveals: Agnostic identification of exchange rate and treasury-market dynamics in inconvenient times
Lucas ter Steege, Sofia Velasco

After the April 2025 US tariff announcements, the US dollar fell and long-term US Treasury yields rose, even though markets were clearly in risk-off mode. That combination is puzzling, because it runs against a standard safe-haven pattern. The authors use high-frequency asset price data and a flexible econometric approach that exploits changes in volatility, allowing the data to reveal the mechanisms at work rather than imposing assumptions in advance. They conclude that the episode cannot be explained as the effect of a single tariff shock. Instead, it reflects the interaction of safe-haven demand, confidence in US institutions, Treasury-market intermediation and changes in the convenience value of dollar-denominated safe assets. Together, these forces help explain why the dollar weakened despite an initial flight to safety.

Key takeaways

  • The episode cannot be understood as the effect of a single tariff shock.
  • Safe-haven demand, confidence in US institutions, Treasury-market intermediation and the convenience value of dollar safe assets interacted.
  • The analysis uses high-frequency asset price data and changes in volatility to identify mechanisms without imposing a rigid structure.
Why it matters for markets. The paper cautions against reading the dollar and Treasury moves after the tariff announcements as a simple response to one shock. It points to safe-haven demand, confidence in US institutions, Treasury-market intermediation and the convenience value of dollar safe assets as separate drivers that can pull in different directions. For FX and rates strategists, the dollar's safe-haven behaviour in risk-off episodes may be less reliable than usually assumed.

Market focus: DM. The paper studies the US dollar and US Treasury market, so it is centred on an advanced economy.

Transversal  International organisation  ·  IMF Working Papers  ·  24 Oct 2026

US bond supply shocks lift foreign yields and weigh on global output

Global Spillovers from U.S. Bond Supply Shocks
Danila Smirnov

The author studies how shocks to the supply of US government bonds spill over to the rest of the world. The shocks are identified from yield moves around US Treasury auction announcements and then used as instruments in daily and monthly local projections (a regression technique that traces effects over time) for advanced and emerging economies. A debt expansion shock that raises the US 10-year yield by 10 basis points lifts foreign 10-year yields by roughly 7 basis points within a few business days. Monthly results show contractionary real effects: US industrial production falls by up to 0.4 percent after eight months, and foreign industrial production falls by a comparable amount, with a more persistent profile. The foreign contraction reflects financial tightening, exchange-rate depreciation and weaker US demand. Spillovers are amplified by foreign investor participation and weak domestic fundamentals.

Key takeaways

  • A shock lifting the US 10-year yield by 10 basis points raises foreign 10-year yields by roughly 7 basis points within days.
  • US and foreign industrial production both fall by around 0.4 percent, with a more persistent decline abroad.
  • Moving from the 25th to the 75th percentile of foreign investor participation deepens the industrial production decline by 0.5 percentage points.
Why it matters for markets. Treasury supply emerges as a channel through which US fiscal financing needs can tighten global financial conditions, with pass-through to long-term rates in both advanced and emerging economies. For rates strategists, auction announcements are a source of foreign-yield risk. For FX and emerging-market views, the results point to depreciation pressure and larger real effects where foreign investor participation is higher.

Market focus: Transversal. The paper traces US bond supply spillovers to advanced and emerging economies together, so it spans both groups.

Transversal  NBER working paper  ·  NBER Working Papers  ·  20 Oct 2026

US tightening raises global risk premia by hitting dollar-short arbitrageurs

A Model of U.S. Monetary Policy and the Global Financial Cycle
Rohan Kekre, Moritz Lenel

The authors ask why US monetary policy moves global asset prices, and build a general equilibrium model to answer. In it, US policy affects global risk premia by revaluing the wealth of arbitrageurs whose assets and liabilities are in different currencies. If their portfolios are mean-variance efficient (optimally balancing expected return against risk), these arbitrageurs must be short the dollar. A US tightening therefore erodes their net worth and raises the global price of risk. The authors discipline the mechanism so that it rationalises the effects of US monetary policy on international asset prices, and they quantify the macroeconomic spillovers and the role of policy responses abroad. They also consider a possible future with higher dollar interest rates, in which arbitrageurs' dollar funding and US monetary policy spillovers are dampened.

Key takeaways

  • Currency-mismatched arbitrageurs must be short the dollar if their portfolios are mean-variance efficient, which makes their net worth sensitive to US policy.
  • The model rationalises effects of US monetary policy on international asset prices and quantifies macroeconomic spillovers and the role of policy responses abroad.
  • With higher dollar interest rates in future, arbitrageurs' dollar funding and US monetary policy spillovers would be dampened.
Why it matters for markets. The paper offers a balance-sheet explanation for the global financial cycle, in which US rate changes transmit through the global price of risk and the dollar rather than only through local rates. That is relevant to how FX, credit and emerging-market assets react to US monetary tightening. The result that higher dollar rates could dampen spillovers suggests the sensitivity may change over time.

Market focus: Transversal. The paper is a theoretical general equilibrium model of global spillovers with no single economy as its focus.

Euro area inflation risks have different drivers at short and long horizons

Understanding inflation: insights from the term structure of inflation risks

The paper asks what the term structure of inflation risks reveals about inflation dynamics. Using prices of traded zero-coupon inflation caps and floors, the authors develop a non-parametric method, combined with a Student's t-copula (a statistical tool linking distributions across horizons), to estimate risk-neutral densities, which are market-implied probability distributions for inflation, at short, medium and long horizons. Long-horizon risks are identified from liquid instruments alone, at daily frequency, without forward-starting contracts. Focusing on the euro area over 2009-2026, they find that the term structure of risks carries information on how persistent inflation shocks are and how far changes in the outlook become embedded at longer horizons. Short- and medium-term risks relate mainly to current inflation, confidence indicators, commodity prices and near-term macroeconomic risks, while long-term risks are more closely tied to monetary and financial conditions.

Key takeaways

  • The term structure of inflation risks shows how persistent inflation shocks are and how far outlook changes become embedded at longer horizons.
  • Short- and medium-term risks are mainly associated with current inflation, confidence indicators, commodity prices and near-term macroeconomic risks.
  • Long-term risks are more strongly related to monetary and financial conditions, so relying on a single maturity gives an incomplete picture.
Why it matters for markets. Densities from inflation caps and floors give a market-based read on how inflation risks vary across horizons. For euro rates and inflation-linked strategists, the findings suggest that looking at the whole term structure of risks, rather than one maturity, helps in judging whether shocks are becoming embedded. Long-horizon risks tied to monetary and financial conditions link inflation pricing to policy and funding backdrops.

Market focus: DM. The paper analyses euro area inflation options, so it is centred on an advanced economy.

EM  International organisation  ·  IMF Working Papers  ·  2 Oct 2026

Stablecoin use rises after capital controls and currency crises and persists

International Stablecoin Flows, Capital Controls and Currency Crises
Marco Reuter

The paper builds a country-level panel of estimated international stablecoin flows covering 188 countries, totalling $21.4 trillion in gross flows between 2018 and 2025. Activity is disproportionately concentrated in emerging markets and developing economies, which hold three quarters of stablecoins. The authors then examine how flows respond to policy and crisis events. When countries restrict capital outflows, stablecoin activity rises by roughly 30 percent over the following six months, then plateaus, and does not fall back when restrictions are removed. When currency crises hit, stablecoin holdings increase by roughly 75 percent within a year, and the new holdings persist. Both findings suggest that dollarisation may now also operate through stablecoins.

Key takeaways

  • Estimated gross international stablecoin flows across 188 countries total $21.4 trillion between 2018 and 2025, with emerging and developing economies holding three quarters.
  • Stablecoin activity rises by roughly 30 percent within six months of capital outflow restrictions and does not fall back when they are removed.
  • Stablecoin holdings increase by roughly 75 percent within a year of a currency crisis, and the new holdings persist.
Why it matters for markets. The findings suggest stablecoins may add a channel for dollarisation that persists after controls are lifted or crises pass. For emerging-market FX and sovereign strategists, this raises the question of how well capital controls and conventional flow data capture pressure on local currencies. It also points to a possible source of persistent dollar demand in countries under stress.

Market focus: EM. Stablecoin holdings are concentrated in emerging and developing economies, and capital controls and currency crises are the central mechanisms.

DM  Regional Federal Reserve Bank  ·  Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago  ·  1 Oct 2026

Monetary-policy risk is priced across US bonds and equities

Monetary-Policy Risk and Equilibrium Asset Prices
Thomas B. King, Corey Feldman

The paper quantifies how uncertainty about the conduct of monetary policy affects asset prices. Nominal yields, real yields, stock returns and implied volatilities all vary more on days with Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) communication than on FOMC blackout days, consistent with policy communication being a source of risk, and they respond significantly to changes in interest-rate uncertainty around announcements. A New Keynesian model with stochastic variation in the policy rule quantitatively matches this evidence. In the model, monetary-policy risk contributes to excess returns on equities and nominal bonds and accounts for much of the risk premium on inflation-indexed bonds. Transitory increases lower stock and bond prices, reduce inflation-risk premia and generate macroeconomic weakness similar to aggregate-demand shocks. The effective lower bound reduces the size of the risk but magnifies its asset-pricing consequences.

Key takeaways

  • Asset prices vary more on FOMC communication days than on blackout days, consistent with policy communication being a source of risk.
  • In the model, monetary-policy risk contributes to excess returns on equities and nominal bonds and accounts for much of the inflation-indexed bond risk premium.
  • The effective lower bound reduces the size of monetary-policy risk but magnifies its asset-pricing consequences, consistent with the data.
Why it matters for markets. The results frame uncertainty about the policy rule, not just the expected rate path, as a priced risk for US nominal and real yields, equities and implied volatility. Transitory rises in this risk lower stock and bond prices and mimic aggregate-demand weakness, which is useful context for reading announcement-day moves. The authors also use the model to assess the increase in risk after the rapid tightening of 2022.

Market focus: DM. The paper studies US FOMC communication and US asset prices using a model calibrated to US data.

European sovereign-bank link persists but depends on holdings, accounting and fiscal capacity

When and for Whom Does the Sovereign-Bank Nexus Matter? Evidence from Post-Crisis Europe

The paper asks whether sovereign risk still affects banks a decade after Europe began reshaping its financial architecture. The authors combine daily bank and sovereign credit default swap (CDS) spreads with supervisory data on banks' domestic sovereign portfolios. They find that the sovereign-bank relationship remains economically meaningful but is highly conditional. Banks with larger sovereign holdings respond more sharply to home-sovereign risk, and this amplification is substantially stronger for mark-to-market than for amortised-cost exposures, consistent with more immediate recognition of valuation losses. Transmission from sovereign to bank is stronger for less-capitalised, wholesale-funded and peripheral banks, in periods of elevated market uncertainty, and when the home sovereign has less fiscal capacity. The policy reforms aimed at weakening the link reduced its extent but did not eliminate it.

Key takeaways

  • Banks with larger domestic sovereign holdings respond more sharply to home-sovereign risk, especially for mark-to-market rather than amortised-cost exposures.
  • Transmission is stronger for less-capitalised, wholesale-funded and peripheral banks, in uncertain markets, and when the sovereign has less fiscal capacity.
  • Post-crisis policy reforms reduced the extent of the sovereign-bank link but did not eliminate it.
Why it matters for markets. For European credit and sovereign strategists, the paper shows that sovereign stress can still feed into bank CDS spreads, most strongly for weaker, wholesale-funded and peripheral banks. The conditionality suggests bank spreads may be most sensitive to sovereign spreads when markets are uncertain and fiscal space is thin. Accounting treatment of sovereign holdings is a further variable to track when assessing bank exposure.

Market focus: DM. The paper covers European banks and their home sovereigns across euro area core and peripheral economies, which are advanced economies.

Transversal  NBER working paper  ·  NBER Working Papers  ·  6 Oct 2026

Greater uncertainty shrinks and can remove multiple equilibria in sovereign debt markets

Debt Sustainability and the Range of Multiple Equilibria
Olivier Blanchard, Pedro Martínez-Bruera, Gonzalo Huertas

The paper studies how large the range of multiple equilibria is in sovereign debt markets. The authors show that the nature of the equilibrium depends crucially on uncertainty, modelled first as uncertainty about primary balances and later about the fiscal feedback rule. When uncertainty is low, the range can be large, but it shrinks as uncertainty grows. If uncertainty is large enough, multiplicity disappears and the interest rate is unique and a continuous function of the debt level. The reason is that larger uncertainty weakens the effect of the perceived probability of default on the actual probability of default, reducing and eventually eliminating the scope for multiplicity. In practice, interpreting the source of an interest rate increase is difficult, which complicates central bank intervention.

Key takeaways

  • With low uncertainty the range of multiple equilibria can be large, but it narrows as uncertainty rises.
  • When uncertainty is large enough, the interest rate is unique and varies continuously with the debt level.
  • Larger uncertainty weakens the link between perceived and actual default probabilities, removing the scope for multiplicity.
Why it matters for markets. The paper gives a way to think about when sovereign yields can sit at different levels for the same debt, which is relevant to sovereign credit and rates. The finding that large uncertainty removes multiplicity is a theoretical result, not an empirical forecast. The authors note that it is hard to interpret the source of a rate rise, which complicates central bank intervention in sovereign debt markets.

Market focus: Transversal. The paper is a theoretical study of sovereign debt equilibria with no particular country focus.
