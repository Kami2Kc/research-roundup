Editor's note

This month's papers share a concern with who is on the other side of the market and how that shapes prices. For developed markets, the Federal Reserve work on risk appetite and the Treasury demand system both show that transmission depends on investor behaviour as much as on policy rates or supply alone. The Bank of England and Bank for International Settlements papers extend the point to dealer pricing in dollar funding and to non-bank participation in government debt markets. A second thread concerns external linkages: tariffs and currency invoicing, capital inflows into emerging markets, and Chinese official lending. Together they suggest that aggregate or bilateral measures can hide the margins that move assets.

DM  Regional Federal Reserve Bank  ·  Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco  ·  18 Sep 2026

Fed policy transmits mainly through risk appetite, not the rate path

Risk Appetite and Monetary Transmission
Andreas Schrimpf, Maik Schmeling, Michael D. Bauer

The paper asks how much of US monetary transmission runs through investors' appetite for risk rather than through the expected path of interest rates. The authors build a new high-frequency measure of risk appetite shifts around Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meetings, defined as the common component of changes in risk-sensitive indicators. They find that interest-rate surprises explain only about one-fifth of the variation in risk appetite, so most policy-induced moves in risk asset prices are unrelated to the expected rate path. Using both surprises as external instruments in a proxy structural vector autoregression (SVAR) with two separately identified shocks, they find that risk appetite shocks have large, persistent contractionary effects, lowering output and prices and raising unemployment. Risk-free rate shocks have small, imprecise effects, some with puzzling signs.

Key takeaways

  • Interest-rate surprises explain only about one-fifth of the variation in risk appetite around FOMC meetings.
  • Risk appetite shocks lower output and prices and raise unemployment, with large and persistent effects.
  • Estimates using interest-rate surprises alone miss most transmission because the rate-to-risk-appetite link is state-dependent and Fed communication moves risk appetite independently.
Why it matters for markets. For rates and equity strategists, the results suggest that reading Fed meetings through the policy-rate surprise alone understates the real-economy effect of FOMC decisions. Communication that shifts risk appetite may matter as much as the expected rate path, with the channel running through equity and other risk asset prices. It argues for tracking risk-asset reactions alongside rate repricing, though the findings are for the US.

Market focus: DM. The paper analyses US monetary policy and FOMC announcements and their effects on US risk appetite, asset prices, output and unemployment.

DM  G10 central bank  ·  Federal Reserve Board  ·  18 Sep 2026

Treasury demand is more price-sensitive as foreign official holders retreat

Estimating Yield Impacts of Treasury Demand and Supply Changes

The authors ask how changes in the supply of and demand for US Treasuries move yields. They develop a demand system framework that captures time-varying holdings shares and estimates sectoral demand elasticities using instrumental variables. They find that the Treasury market has become increasingly price-sensitive over time, as less price-sensitive foreign official investors have reduced their participation and more price-sensitive hedge funds and other private investors have gained a larger role. A $100 billion increase in Treasury supply currently raises five-year yields by approximately 3 basis points. The authors validate the model by showing that shifts in the investor base explain a significant portion of historical yield changes. They present the framework as a flexible tool for counterfactuals, including foreign official sales and Federal Reserve balance sheet policies.

Key takeaways

  • A $100 billion rise in Treasury supply currently lifts five-year yields by approximately 3 basis points.
  • Demand has become more price-sensitive as foreign official holders retreat and hedge funds and other private investors gain share.
  • Changes in the investor base explain a significant portion of historical yield changes, which supports the model.
Why it matters for markets. For rates strategists, the estimate offers a benchmark for sizing the yield effect of issuance and for scenarios such as foreign official selling or changes in the Federal Reserve balance sheet. A more price-sensitive investor base implies, other things equal, a smaller yield response per unit of supply, though the abstract gives the effect only for five-year yields. It is a scenario tool, not a forecast.

Market focus: DM. The paper studies US Treasury supply and demand and their effect on yields in the US market.

Transversal  G10 central bank  ·  Bank of England  ·  18 Sep 2026

Synthetic dollar funding is priced differently by each dealer

One dollar, many prices: dealer-specific pricing of synthetic dollar funding

The paper asks whether the price of synthetic dollar funding, obtained through FX forwards, depends on which dealer intermediates the trade. To isolate dealer-specific pricing, the authors compare FX forwards in the same dollar currency pair, half-hour and maturity bucket, which removes the common forward curve. They find that dealer-specific prices vary substantially, with a standard deviation of about 4 basis points per year, both across dealers at a point in time and over time for a given dealer. Dealers charge 2.5 basis points more for buying dollars forward than for selling them, and the gap widens to 11.8 basis points at maturities under one month. The asymmetry survives controls for client-dealer relationships and trade characteristics. Differences in funding costs explain little of the dispersion, pointing instead to dealer clientele and pricing power.

Key takeaways

  • Dealer-specific prices have a standard deviation of about 4 basis points per year within the same pair, half-hour and maturity bucket.
  • Dealers charge 2.5 basis points more to buy dollars forward than to sell them, rising to 11.8 basis points under one month.
  • Dealer funding costs account for little of the dispersion, which reflects clientele and pricing power more than funding conditions.
Why it matters for markets. For FX and rates desks, the findings suggest that a single forward-implied dollar rate can hide meaningful differences in what clients actually pay. Dispersion that reflects dealer pricing power rather than funding costs may matter for execution costs and for how dollar funding conditions are read from market-wide measures. The abstract does not quantify those wider implications.

Market focus: Transversal. The paper looks at dealer pricing of FX forwards in a dollar currency pair, a global market rather than a specific economy group.

Transversal  European central bank  ·  Banco de España  ·  30 Sep 2026

US-China tariff incidence depends on production networks and dollar invoicing

Tariffs, production networks, and spillovers: the case of a US-China Trade War

The paper studies how a US-China tariff war transmits through the economy in the short run. The authors use an open economy, multi-sector New Keynesian model with input-output linkages, sectoral nominal rigidities and heterogeneous currency invoicing. A reciprocal 10 percentage point tariff increase has asymmetric incidence: the tariff-imposing country bears more of the inflationary burden, while the targeted country suffers the larger output contraction. Production networks amplify the contraction, and dollar-denominated border prices weaken the expenditure-switching role of exchange rates, deepening China's downturn. The euro area response is small in aggregate only because trade-diversion gains are offset by weaker demand from China and multilateral adjustments. Imposing tariffs on one Chinese sector at a time shows that incidence is concentrated but aggregate effects cannot be inferred from the tariffed sector alone.

Key takeaways

  • A reciprocal 10 percentage point tariff increase raises inflation more in the tariff imposer and cuts output more in the targeted country.
  • Dollar invoicing weakens exchange-rate expenditure switching, deepening China's contraction compared with producer-currency pricing.
  • The small euro area aggregate response masks offsetting trade-diversion gains and weaker Chinese demand.
Why it matters for markets. For macro and FX strategists, the paper shows that bilateral trade shares alone can give a misleading read of tariff effects. Production networks and dollar invoicing shape both the output hit and the role of exchange rates in adjustment, while a muted euro area aggregate can hide opposing margins. It is a model-based short-run exercise, so results depend on its assumptions.

Market focus: Transversal. The model covers the US, China, the euro area and third-country spillovers, so it is not confined to either emerging or advanced economies.

Transversal  International organisation  ·  Bank for International Settlements  ·  29 Sep 2026

High public debt and non-banks raise government bond market dysfunction risk

High public debt, NBFIs and the risk of market dysfunction
Mathias Drehmann, Sonya Zhu

This paper considers how high government debt and the growing role of non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs) affect the functioning of government debt markets. The abstract points to near record-high government debt levels and a larger non-bank presence as two developments that have reshaped these markets in recent years. It argues that high levels of government sector debt are expected to worsen future market liquidity conditions in general, including by raising the risk of market dysfunction. A large NBFI footprint adds to that risk of dysfunction, yet it also makes very favourable liquidity conditions more likely. The abstract gives no figures, country detail or description of methods, so the evidence behind these conclusions cannot be assessed from it. The message is one of two-sided effects from non-bank participation.

Key takeaways

  • High government debt is expected to worsen future liquidity in government debt markets, including by raising the risk of market dysfunction.
  • A large non-bank footprint heightens the risk of market dysfunction.
  • The same non-bank footprint also makes very favourable liquidity conditions more likely.
Why it matters for markets. For rates and sovereign credit strategists, the paper frames government bond market liquidity as conditional on both debt levels and the investor mix. Non-bank participation may support liquidity in calm periods while raising the risk of dysfunction under stress, which is relevant to how bond liquidity is assessed. The abstract is general and offers no quantification, so it is better read as a framing than as a forecast.

Market focus: Transversal. The paper discusses government debt markets and non-banks in general terms without naming specific economies, implying a cross-country perspective.

EM  NBER working paper  ·  NBER Working Papers  ·  8 Sep 2026

Unexpected capital inflows lift output across 27 emerging economies

Expectations-based Capital Inflow Shocks
Jongrim Ha, Dohan Kim, M. Ayhan Kose, Francis Warnock

Capital inflows respond to expectations about future economic conditions, which makes it hard to identify their effect on output. The authors develop a new measure of capital inflow shocks using an expectations-based framework that isolates the unexpected component of inflows by purging movements predicted by professional forecasters' expectations. Applying it to 27 emerging market economies, they find that capital inflows have sizable expansionary effects on output, working through stronger domestic consumption and investment and easier financing conditions. Equity-type inflows generate greater and more persistent effects than debt-type inflows, and large inflow reversals have more pronounced effects than comparable increases. The authors report that the results are robust to a wide range of alternative specifications.

Key takeaways

  • Unexpected capital inflows have sizable expansionary effects on output across 27 emerging market economies.
  • The effects operate through stronger domestic consumption and investment and easier financing conditions.
  • Equity-type inflows have greater and more persistent effects than debt-type inflows, and large reversals bite harder than comparable increases.
Why it matters for markets. For emerging market strategists, the paper links surprise portfolio flows to growth through consumption, investment and financing conditions, and indicates that composition matters, with equity-type flows mattering more than debt-type flows. The larger effect of reversals suggests that downside risks from sudden stops outweigh the upside from comparable inflows. The abstract reports output effects only.

Market focus: EM. The paper estimates capital inflow shocks and their output effects across 27 emerging market economies.

Euro area stock-bond correlations have drifted toward neutral

When Diversification Fades: a Firm Level Perspective of Stock and Corporate Bond Correlations

The paper examines how euro area corporate bond and stock returns have moved together from 2007 to 2025. Theory suggests correlations should be negative, or at least lower, in market stress and after negative demand shocks, but the evidence shows substantial instability. The authors build equity and corporate bond indices from a strictly matched issuer universe, so the same firms sit in both, and analyse correlation dynamics across major euro area markets using Dynamic Conditional Correlation models. Correlations, historically negative, have gradually trended toward neutrality since 2009 but can shift in either direction rapidly. The discount-rate channel is the dominant driver of co-movement in both low- and high-inflation environments. Financial stress lowers correlations, consistent with flight to quality, only in low-inflation regimes.

Key takeaways

  • Stock-bond correlations in the euro area, historically negative, have trended toward neutrality since 2009 but can swing quickly either way.
  • The discount-rate channel is the dominant driver of co-movement in both low- and high-inflation environments.
  • Financial stress reduces correlations, consistent with flight to quality, only in low-inflation regimes and not in high-inflation ones.
Why it matters for markets. For credit and multi-asset strategists, the results caution against assuming that euro area corporate bonds hedge equity risk. When inflation is high, stress does not reliably lower correlations, and falling discount rates cannot be counted on to favour bonds. Matching issuers across the two indices isolates firm-level co-movement, though the evidence covers only the euro area from 2007 to 2025.

Market focus: DM. The paper studies euro area equity and corporate bond markets, which are advanced economy markets.

EM  NBER working paper  ·  NBER Working Papers  ·  22 Sep 2026

Chinese loans that cannot be defaulted on discipline sovereign bond borrowing

Borrowing in the Shadow of China
Illenin Kondo, César Sosa-Padilla, Zachary Swaziek

China has become a major lender to emerging economies since the early 2000s. The authors argue that its loans have a distinctive structure: borrowers cannot default on the subsidised loans, but can face sudden rollover demands outside their control. They argue this bilateral rollover risk without default risk disciplines borrowing on international markets. In a quantitative long-term sovereign debt model, rollover-prone nondefaultable Chinese loans expose market debt to dilution risk, so governments choose to deleverage from private markets when Chinese inflows arrive. Market debt and bond yield dynamics in the data are consistent with this disciplining effect. The model is then used for two geopolitical scenarios: bloc formation, in which China makes take-it-or-leave-it loans to induce exit from international debt markets, and optimal exposure to China under the risk of a permanent, sudden retrenchment of Chinese funding.

Key takeaways

  • Nondefaultable but rollover-prone Chinese loans push governments to deleverage from international bond markets when Chinese inflows arrive.
  • Observed market debt and bond yield dynamics are consistent with this disciplining effect.
  • Official Chinese finance can improve market borrowing conditions yet become a destabilising force as geopolitical alliances shift.
Why it matters for markets. For emerging market sovereign credit strategists, the paper offers a channel through which bilateral official lending shapes international bond issuance and yields, rather than simply adding to debt. It also highlights a tail risk: a sudden retrenchment of Chinese funding or bloc formation could change borrowers' access to international markets. The results come from a quantitative model and the abstract does not name countries.

Market focus: EM. The paper studies emerging-market sovereign borrowing from China and its effect on international bond markets and yields.
