Editor's note

This month's papers share a concern with who funds whom, and how shocks travel. Three studies look at the dollar and the global financial cycle: foreign demand for Treasuries, the effect of US uncertainty on credit spreads and currencies, and capital flows to low-income countries. Two examine sovereign borrowing, from changing creditor mixes to the risks of bilateral official lending. Two ask how monetary policy transmits, in the euro area and in Brazilian market expectations. The remaining pieces cover the 2025 US tariffs, where an investment boom masked their effect on imports, and what disclosures reveal about opaque private credit.

DM  NBER working paper  ·  NBER Working Papers  ·  3 Aug 2026

Foreign official demand for Treasuries has fallen while private demand has risen

The United States and Its Creditors: Assessing Foreign Demand for U.S. Assets
Anusha Chari, Gian Maria Milesi Ferretti

The paper asks who finances the United States and how foreign demand for US assets, especially Treasury securities, has changed. The authors document shifts in US bilateral external positions across regions and asset classes, producing estimates of creditor positions closer to a nationality-based concept. They find that rising US equity prices explain a sizeable share of the deterioration in the net external position, but foreign purchases of Treasuries have been the largest source of current account financing. The foreign official share of Treasuries has fallen sharply over the past decade, while the private share, especially through financial centres, has risen. Lower reserve accumulation, larger Federal Reserve holdings and dollar appreciation explain the official decline. Geoeconomic fragmentation is negatively correlated with official demand, while private demand stays sensitive to safe-haven dynamics.

Key takeaways

  • Foreign purchases of Treasuries have been the largest source of US current account financing, even as equity price gains drove much of the weaker net position.
  • The decline in official holdings is explained by lower reserve accumulation, higher Federal Reserve holdings and dollar appreciation against other reserve currencies.
  • Geoeconomic fragmentation is negatively correlated with official Treasury demand, whereas foreign private demand remains sensitive to safe-haven dynamics.
Why it matters for markets. A marginal Treasury buyer that is increasingly private and routed through financial centres may respond more to risk sentiment than to reserve policy. That bears on how rates strategists think about term premia and on dollar views during geopolitical or fiscal stress. The findings describe structural shifts and do not by themselves forecast yields.

Market focus: DM. The paper analyses the United States as the only economy, focusing on demand for US Treasuries and other US assets.

Transversal  G10 central bank  ·  Federal Reserve Board  ·  31 Aug 2026

Higher US return uncertainty widens credit spreads and strengthens the dollar

Pricing Risk Globally: Intermediary Constraints, the Dollar, and the Global Financial Cycle

Akinci, Kalemli-Ozcan and Queralto ask how greater uncertainty about US asset returns affects global asset prices and exchange rates. They combine empirical evidence with a two-country model in which financial intermediaries face balance-sheet constraints. Empirically, uncertainty shocks widen global credit spreads, appreciate the dollar and raise currency risk premia. In the model, higher uncertainty tightens intermediary constraints and lowers asset prices, which reverses the counterfactual rise in asset prices that frictionless models produce. Because constraints make net worth especially valuable in bad times, risk premia respond strongly to uncertainty shocks. This interaction allows the model to match the observed responses of credit spreads, the currency premium and the dollar. The paper thus offers a mechanism linking US uncertainty to financial conditions abroad.

Key takeaways

  • Empirically, uncertainty about US asset returns widens global credit spreads, appreciates the dollar and increases currency risk premia.
  • Intermediary balance-sheet constraints make higher uncertainty lower asset prices, whereas frictionless models predict the opposite.
  • Net worth is especially valuable in bad times under constraints, so risk premia respond strongly and the model fits the data.
Why it matters for markets. The paper gives a structural channel for the familiar pattern in which US-centred uncertainty coincides with wider credit spreads and a stronger dollar. For credit and FX strategists, it supports treating dollar strength and spread widening as linked responses to intermediary constraints rather than separate events. It is a model-based result and does not forecast any specific episode.

Market focus: Transversal. The two-country model links US uncertainty to credit spreads and the dollar across both US and foreign economies, so it is cross-country rather than specific to one market group.

DM  G10 central bank  ·  Banque de France  ·  31 Aug 2026

Euro area monetary policy transmission is broadly homogeneous, with crisis-driven divergences

Is Monetary Policy Transmission Heterogeneous across Euro Area Countries and over Time? A Reassessment

The paper reassesses whether monetary policy transmission differs across euro area countries and over time, covering 2000–2025. The authors use monthly local projections to estimate effects of monetary policy on many transmission variables at euro area and national levels, then check the results with a factor-augmented vector autoregression (FAVAR). They find limited heterogeneity in output and inflation responses, despite asymmetric responses in mortgage-market variables. Structural differences such as household indebtedness, debt maturity, interest rate rigidity and sectoral composition shape national responses, but they partly offset one another. Asymmetry is greater for sovereign spreads, food prices, credit variables and a consumption proxy. Rolling-window estimates show divergences are temporary and crisis-driven, reverting to a low baseline. Unconventional monetary policies tend to reduce divergences in crises, acting more strongly on the hardest-hit countries.

Key takeaways

  • Output and inflation responses to monetary policy shocks show limited heterogeneity across euro area countries, although mortgage-market variables respond asymmetrically.
  • Household debt, debt maturity, rate rigidity and sectoral composition each matter for national transmission, but they partly offset one another.
  • Time-varying divergences are temporary and crisis-driven, and unconventional policies tend to narrow them, most strongly in the countries most affected.
Why it matters for markets. For euro area rates and macro views, the results suggest a single ECB policy stance transmits to output and inflation fairly evenly across members. Differences appear more in sovereign spreads, credit variables and mortgage channels, which matter for periphery spread and bank-credit views. Divergences look crisis-related rather than structural, though the evidence rests on estimated models.

Market focus: DM. The paper studies monetary policy transmission across euro area member countries, all of which are advanced economies.

Stronger dollar linked to weaker private capital inflows to low-income countries

Riding the Global Financial Cycle
Alexei Miksjuk, Yipei Zhang

As low-income countries gain access to international capital markets, financing opportunities grow but so does exposure to external shocks. The paper asks how far the global financial cycle drives capital flows to these countries and whether government borrowing responds to it. The authors present empirical results for the period after the global financial crisis. They find that the global financial cycle has been a significant driver of private capital flows to low-income countries, with a stronger US dollar against advanced economy currencies associated with weaker net inflows. External government borrowing had a statistically significant but relatively small counter-cyclical component, moving inversely to global financial and economic cycles, which complements policy responses to shocks. The abstract does not give further detail on the data or estimation approach.

Key takeaways

  • Since the global financial crisis, the global financial cycle has significantly driven private capital flows to low-income countries.
  • A stronger US dollar against advanced economy currencies was associated with weaker net private inflows to these countries.
  • External government borrowing had a small but statistically significant counter-cyclical component, moving inversely to global financial and economic cycles.
Why it matters for markets. The paper reinforces that dollar strength is a key external driver of funding conditions for frontier and low-income borrowers. For emerging market strategists, it supports watching the dollar when assessing private inflows and external financing risks. The counter-cyclical element in government borrowing is small, so it should not be read as a strong buffer against shocks.

Market focus: EM. The paper studies private capital flows and government external borrowing in low-income countries, which are developing economies exposed to US dollar cycles.

DM  Regional Federal Reserve Bank  ·  Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis  ·  7 Aug 2026

AI-driven investment boom masked tariff effects on US imports in 2025

Tariffs, Investment, and the Missing Trade Collapse
Andrea Raffo, Michael E. Waugh, Andrea Prestipino, Francesco Ferrante

US tariff rates in 2025 reached levels not seen since the Great Depression, yet imports increased. The authors build an open-economy New Keynesian model with tariff heterogeneity, inventories and shocks to investment that capture the boom driven by artificial intelligence (AI). The model matches the untargeted paths of imports, output and inflation, and is used to separate the effects of tariffs from those of the investment boom. Without the boom, imports would have fallen by 10 percent and activity would have contracted by 0.7 percent. The effects of tariffs depend on which goods are tariffed: tariffs on consumption goods and intermediates act like supply shocks, while tariffs on capital goods act like demand shocks. Because the 2025 increases were concentrated on consumption goods, output damage was limited but the inflationary impulse was amplified.

Key takeaways

  • Absent the investment boom, imports would have fallen by 10 percent and activity would have contracted by 0.7 percent.
  • Tariffs on consumption goods and intermediates behave like supply shocks, while tariffs on capital goods behave like demand shocks.
  • Concentrating tariffs on consumption goods and sparing capital goods limited the output damage but amplified the inflationary impulse.
Why it matters for markets. The paper helps explain why trade data did not collapse and why tariffs show up more in prices than in output. For US macro and rates views, the composition of tariffs and the strength of AI-related investment matter for the growth and inflation mix. The conclusions depend on a calibrated model, so they are a decomposition rather than a forecast.

Market focus: DM. The paper models the US economy's response to the 2025 tariffs and an AI-driven investment boom, so it concerns an advanced economy.

DM  Regional Federal Reserve Bank  ·  Federal Reserve Bank of Boston  ·  5 Aug 2026

Business development company filings offer a window into opaque US private credit

Early Warning Signals in Private Credit? What BDC Portfolios Reveal about Emerging Risks
J. Christina Wang, Leslie Sheng Shen, José Fillat

Private credit, meaning lending by nonbank financial institutions, has grown to over $1 trillion in the United States, yet most of it is opaque because private credit funds need not publish their holdings. Business development companies (BDCs) are an exception: they are publicly registered investment vehicles that must file quarterly and annual reports with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The authors use these disclosures as a window into the wider segment. They analyse trends in the pricing of BDC loans, the creditworthiness of borrowers and the industry composition of BDC loan portfolios, with the aim of detecting emerging risks. The abstract does not report the specific findings, so readers should consult the paper for the results and for how representative BDCs are of private credit as a whole.

Key takeaways

  • US private credit has grown to over $1 trillion, yet most of it remains opaque to investors and policymakers.
  • BDCs file periodic reports with the Securities and Exchange Commission, which makes them an exception among private credit vehicles.
  • The analysis covers loan pricing, borrower creditworthiness and the industry composition of BDC loan portfolios to look for early warning signals.
Why it matters for markets. Public BDC filings are one of few observable data sources on private credit, which matters for credit strategists tracking risk outside banks and public bond markets. Trends in loan pricing and borrower quality could inform views on the wider credit cycle. The abstract states no results, and BDCs may not represent the whole segment.

Market focus: DM. The paper studies US business development companies and US private credit, which are lending markets in an advanced economy.

Bilateral official lending can erode market discipline and amplify debt dilution

The Perils of Bilateral Sovereign Debt
Francisco Roldan, César Sosa-Padilla

Motivated by the rise of official creditors outside the Paris Club framework, the paper studies how senior official lending interacts with market debt. The authors develop a quantitative sovereign default model with a large senior lender with whom borrowing terms are negotiated. Raising more net financing from the market strengthens the government's bargaining position and improves its bilateral terms. This endogenous cross-elasticity erodes the discipline normally imposed by spreads and amplifies debt dilution, producing welfare losses even though bilateral borrowing helps avert some defaults after the fact. With pre-specified rules, rewarding market issuance can be enough to generate such losses, whereas an optimal rule raises bilateral rates as net market financing rises. The direction of the cross-elasticity could therefore guide assessment of new forms of bilateral sovereign debt.

Key takeaways

  • Borrowing more from markets improves a government's bargaining position and its bilateral terms, creating a cross-elasticity between market and official debt.
  • This cross-elasticity weakens the disciplining role of spreads and amplifies debt dilution, causing welfare losses despite some defaults being averted.
  • An optimal rule raises bilateral rates with net market financing, while rules that reward market issuance can be enough to generate losses.
Why it matters for markets. As official creditors outside the Paris Club grow, the design of bilateral loans may change how sovereign spreads signal risk and how dilution affects existing bondholders. This is relevant to emerging and frontier sovereign credit analysis and restructuring expectations. The model is theoretical and not calibrated to a specific country, so it informs assessment rather than pricing.

Market focus: Transversal. The paper presents a theoretical sovereign default model that is not calibrated to any specific country, so its lessons apply across borrowers.

Brazilian market forecasts imply weak monetary transmission and fiscal unresponsiveness to debt

Inferring Structural Beliefs from Macroeconomic Expectations
Philip Barrett

The paper analyses multi-horizon market forecasts for Brazilian macroeconomic variables from 2010 to 2026. A structural macroeconomic model interprets the stated forecasts as the outcome of a coherent belief system, yielding time-varying beliefs about policy rules, transmission mechanisms and structural shocks. Beliefs about the monetary policy rule vary in two distinct dimensions, with the perceived target and the Taylor response coefficients moving independently. Markets see monetary transmission as weak, with very flat perceived Phillips and investment-saving (IS) curves, and see fiscal policy as increasingly unresponsive to higher debt. After an unexpected monetary tightening, the perceived inflation target is unchanged but the perceived response to inflation increases, with larger effects for monetary surprises and smaller for news shocks.

Key takeaways

  • Perceived inflation targets and Taylor response coefficients vary independently, so beliefs about the monetary policy rule have two distinct dimensions.
  • Markets see monetary transmission as weak, with very flat perceived Phillips and IS curves in Brazil.
  • Markets increasingly see fiscal policy as unresponsive to higher debt, and monetary surprises raise the perceived response to inflation more than news shocks.
Why it matters for markets. The paper offers a way to read survey and market forecasts as beliefs about policy behaviour, relevant to Brazilian rates and inflation expectations. Perceived weak transmission and fiscal unresponsiveness bear on views of the policy rate path and sovereign risk. The findings are model-dependent interpretations of forecasts, not direct measures of policy effectiveness.

Market focus: EM. The paper studies market beliefs about monetary and fiscal policy in Brazil, an emerging market.
